# Section rules

These are functional checks, not fixed templates. Judge whether each section performs its scientific job with the supplied evidence.
For cross-section claim-evidence alignment, promise-delivery and repeated-value consistency, use [cross-manuscript-checks.md](cross-manuscript-checks.md) as the authoritative rule set. The checks below concern each section's distinct function.

## Title

Check that the title:

- expresses the central contribution, phenomenon, or innovation rather than merely saying a study was conducted;
- removes empty lead-ins such as `Research on`, `Study on`, or `Investigation on` when they add no scientific meaning;
- uses specific nouns and verbs rather than vague praise such as `simple`, `robust`, `efficient`, or `important` without a meaningful qualifier;
- contains enough qualifiers to avoid overselling the scope;
- avoids `first`, `best`, `highest`, `record`, or equivalent claims unless the manuscript and search basis genuinely support them and the wording is appropriately bounded.

Treat title length as a heuristic, not a hard rule. See `editorial-heuristics.md`.

## Abstract

The Abstract should function as a compact version of the paper's scientific result and implication.

Check whether it contains, in an economical sequence:

1. the problem or exact gap when needed;
2. the approach or experiment at the level needed for interpretation;
3. the decisive result(s);
4. the conclusion or bounded implication / outlook.

Check that:

- background does not consume the abstract at the expense of results;
- every sentence adds new information rather than repeating the same time range, method label, or conclusion;
- headline numbers are interpretable with direction, unit, scale, comparison basis, and uncertainty where relevant;
- abbreviations, references, figures, and tables follow the supplied journal rules; without journal rules, flag only obvious readability problems rather than inventing prohibitions.

## Introduction

Audit the Introduction as a funnel with four functional levels:

1. **System need / broad importance**: why the problem matters scientifically.
2. **Established knowledge / representative prior work**: what is already known and which typical studies define the field.
3. **Exact unresolved gap**: what remains unknown, why it remains unresolved, and why that gap matters.
4. **This paper's move**: what the present work does to answer the gap, with a short bounded significance statement when useful.

Check that:

- references are synthesized by scientific role rather than listed as a catalogue of authors;
- representative or field-defining work is not evaded merely to make the present paper look novel;
- prior work is criticized precisely and respectfully rather than dismissed wholesale;
- mechanism detail appears only when it changes the research logic, hypothesis, variable choice, or interpretation;
- the core action and object of the present study appear clearly, without being buried inside long method insertions;
- an interdisciplinary Introduction preserves one dominant direction and explains why the second field is necessary rather than giving several disconnected mini-reviews;
- the final research move directly answers the stated gap rather than opening a different problem.

## Results

Treat Results as a figure-led scientific argument rather than an experiment log.

Check that:

- subsections separate meaningful experimental or analytical units;
- each subsection reaches a result quickly, normally after no more than the minimal analytical setup needed to understand it;
- each main figure or panel has a clear role and its main content is described in the text;
- a figure, table, or scientific point is not repeatedly re-explained in distant locations unless a later reference serves a genuinely different function;
- result order progresses logically, often from simpler observation to harder explanation, mechanism, boundary, or consequence;
- the text does not repeatedly reintroduce background, novelty, or motivation that should already have been established in the Introduction;
- the reason for an experiment or analysis is explained when it helps the reader understand the logic, while procedural detail remains in Methods;
- every major result that drives the paper appears in the manuscript's main argument, and nonessential results are considered for Supplementary Information rather than forced into the main text.

## Discussion / Conclusion

Audit the Discussion as a scientific interpretation and closure section.

Check that it:

1. synthesizes the most important finding before restating methods;
2. explains major experimental or analytical results;
3. directly addresses surprising or expectation-inconsistent results;
4. distinguishes supported explanation from plausible but unverified alternatives;
5. compares prior work along explicit dimensions such as region, scale, season, sample, metric, lifecycle, or method rather than saying only `consistent with previous studies`;
6. states limitations as source -> affected conclusion -> robustness already shown -> residual uncertainty -> possible resolution;
7. discusses future relevance and application without hiding known constraints;
8. returns to the central Introduction gap so the paper closes the scientific loop.
When a major result cannot be uniquely explained, it is acceptable to present multiple evidence-grounded interpretations. Do not convert them into a single claimed mechanism merely for narrative smoothness.

## Methods

Check whether Methods allow a knowledgeable reader to understand and, where feasible, reproduce the reported analysis.

Review for:

- data identity, coverage, inclusion/exclusion, preprocessing, and derived variables;
- experimental or observational setup;
- calculation, model, simulation, statistical, or reconstruction definitions;
- parameters, thresholds, uncertainty treatment, and sensitivity choices needed to interpret the Results;
- enough detail for the exact metrics and figures used in the manuscript;
- objective, direct prose rather than promotional language;
- sensible separation between essential Methods and highly specialized material moved to Supplementary Information.

Do not claim the Methods are reproducible when the underlying code/data are unavailable. Judge only whether the manuscript reports the information expected by its own analyses and supplied journal requirements.

## Figures, tables, and captions

Check that:

- main figures form the same logical sequence as the Results;
- each panel contributes to the figure's central question;
- panel order and text order agree;
- scales, units, signs, labels, abbreviations, symbols, sample sizes, uncertainty notation, and color meanings are defined consistently;
- captions let the figure be interpreted without forcing the reader to reconstruct basic definitions elsewhere;
- clarity takes priority over decoration;
- figure design does not hide uncertainty, comparability, or important negative/null information;
- the text and caption do not contradict one another.

## References

Check that:

- the supplied target-journal formatting rules are followed when available;
- representative prior work is cited where scientifically necessary;
- citations for novelty, priority, record performance or a known field limitation receive special attention under [cross-manuscript-checks.md](cross-manuscript-checks.md). In manuscript-only review, do not certify source content.

Do not perform a global literature or novelty audit here; route that need to Nature Story or a dedicated literature workflow.

## Acknowledgements and authorship-facing text

Check only what the supplied manuscript and target-journal instructions support:

- acknowledgements should identify genuine contributions or support rather than operate as generic praise;
- names, grants, institutions, and contribution descriptions should be internally consistent;
- authorship, contributor consent, conflicts of interest, and acknowledgement consent are journal/institutional policy matters. Do not treat a lecture-specific anecdote as a universal ethics rule.

## General sentence and paragraph checks

Across all sections:

- keep one main scientific judgement per sentence even when evidence and a short interpretation appear together;
- prefer common disciplinary wording over decorative synonyms;
- use transitions that express the actual logical relation rather than inserting connectors mechanically;
- remove repeated statements that add no new role;
- preserve exact spatial, temporal, vertical, population, experimental, model, and statistical boundaries;
- quantify adjectives such as `substantial`, `strong`, `remarkable`, or `dramatic` when they materially affect interpretation;
- prioritize clarity over stylistic display.
