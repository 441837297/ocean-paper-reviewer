# Cross-manuscript checks

## 1. Claim-evidence alignment

For every central claim, identify its evidence type:

- direct observation / measurement;
- computed result;
- statistical association;
- causal identification;
- model attribution;
- mechanistic inference;
- projection / scenario;
- interpretation / hypothesis.

Flag any wording whose strength exceeds the evidence type. In particular:

- association is not causation;
- a spatial pattern is not automatically a mechanism;
- a model response is not automatically an observed real-world effect;
- a regional or period-specific result is not automatically global or timeless;
- a subgroup effect cannot replace a weaker whole-sample conclusion without stating both levels;
- a trend does not become a conclusion merely because it visually appears directional.
- positive and negative local-trend area fractions alone do not demonstrate cancellation in an independently fitted regional or global aggregate trend. Assess signed magnitudes, weighting, coverage and aggregation method before making that inference; without them, describe the local trends as spatially heterogeneous or mixed in sign. This is a claim-evidence check, not a ban on spatial analysis.

## 2. Number and definition consistency

Search all supplied materials for repeated core values and definitions:

- headline effect sizes;
- means, trends, percentages, ratios, confidence intervals, p-values, uncertainty ranges;
- time windows;
- spatial domains;
- variable names and symbols;
- sample counts;
- denominators and baselines;
- sign conventions;
- units and unit conversions;
- model or experiment labels.

When two values differ, do not decide which is correct unless the authoritative source is clear. Flag the conflict and identify all locations.

## 3. Promise-delivery consistency

Compare:

- Title promise vs Abstract result;
- Abstract result vs Results evidence;
- Introduction gap vs Results analysis;
- Results claim vs Discussion explanation;
- Discussion implication vs stated limitation;
- Conclusion vs body evidence;
- Cover letter / highlights / graphical abstract vs manuscript, if supplied.

A statement can be individually plausible yet still be a preflight problem when another section frames the same result differently.

## 4. Figure-text consistency

For each main figure, check:

- the text cites the correct figure/panel;
- the caption's labels, units, signs, groups, and uncertainty match the plotted content;
- the main textual claim is visible in the figure or in the analysis clearly linked to it;
- a result is not described more strongly in prose than the figure supports;
- figure ordering follows the stated analytical logic;
- the same figure is not made to carry incompatible functions in distant sections.

## 5. Citation alignment

In a manuscript-only review, check citation placement, intended function and whether the attached claim appears matched to that function. Prefer clause-level mapping when a sentence makes several scientific subclaims. Do not state that a cited source actually supports a claim without reading that source. When the source is supplied or independently accessed, compare its findings and scope with the claim; topical similarity alone does not establish support.

## 6. Terminology discipline

Flag:

- one concept described by multiple near-synonyms that could imply different definitions;
- one term used for variables with different operational definitions;
- changed abbreviations or capitalization;
- inconsistent mechanism labels;
- ambiguous pronouns or generic references such as `this effect` when several candidate effects are nearby.

## 7. Redundancy and section leakage

Common leakage patterns:

- Introduction reports detailed results;
- Results re-explain broad motivation and novelty;
- Discussion repeats entire Results paragraphs instead of interpreting them;
- Methods contains promotional claims;
- Abstract introduces a challenge or mechanism absent from the main paper;
- Conclusion introduces a new claim not established earlier.

Flag the misplaced function and recommend relocation or deletion rather than simply rewriting the sentence.
