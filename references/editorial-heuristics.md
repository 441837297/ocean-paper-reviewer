# Editorial heuristics

These rules were presented in the source lecture as practical writing advice. Treat them as **soft diagnostics**, not universal scientific requirements. Use `HEURISTIC` unless the manuscript also has a real clarity, logic, or consistency defect.

## Title length

The lecture recommends that a strong title can often stay within roughly **15 words**.

Use this only to trigger a concision check. A longer title can pass when the qualifiers are scientifically necessary. A shorter title can fail when it removes a necessary scope or method qualifier and thereby oversells the paper.

## Paragraph length

The lecture cites a practical warning for paragraphs above roughly **250 words**.

Do not fail a paragraph solely for length. Ask whether it contains multiple functions, hides a logical break, or overloads the reader. A long but single-purpose paragraph can remain; a shorter paragraph can still be incoherent.

## Main figure count

The lecture suggests roughly **4-6 main figures** and encourages richer, logically grouped subpanels rather than many disconnected simple figures.

Treat this as a narrative-economy check. Science determines the final number. Flag figure proliferation only when it reflects fragmented logic, repeated evidence, or poor grouping.

## Number of storylines / highlights

The lecture strongly prefers a focused paper with one dominant line and a small number of major highlights, often framed as roughly **1-3**.

Do not count bullets mechanically. Flag only when the manuscript genuinely asks the reader to follow several independent papers at once, or when many claimed highlights cannot be reduced to a coherent common contribution.

## Tense simplification

The lecture recommends simplifying tense choices for authors who struggle with English and keeping tense use locally consistent.

Treat this as a clarity strategy, not a universal grammar law. Do not force all Methods/Results into one tense when disciplinary or journal convention requires otherwise. Flag tense only when it obscures time, sequence, or scientific status.

## Abstract abbreviations, citations, figures, and tables

The lecture advises avoiding them in abstracts unless the journal format supports them.

Do not impose this without journal context. Flag only unreadable abbreviations or clear conflicts with supplied journal requirements.

## `first`, `best`, `highest`, `record`, and similar priority claims

The lecture warns strongly against overselling and suggests cautious qualification such as `to our knowledge` when such wording is genuinely necessary.

A qualifier does not make an unsupported priority claim acceptable. Require evidence appropriate to the claim and, when novelty itself is under review, recommend a literature/novelty audit rather than accepting the phrase.

## Vocabulary and syntax

The lecture advises:

- prefer simple sentence structures when possible;
- use common disciplinary expressions rather than decorative synonyms;
- verify unfamiliar terminology against scholarly usage rather than relying on general-language search alone;
- choose logical connectors precisely;
- prefer concise wording;
- prioritize clarity over visual or verbal ornament.

Apply these as writing-quality diagnostics, not rigid word bans.

## Visual design

The lecture's hierarchy is **clarity before beautification**.

Flag a visually attractive figure only when design choices interfere with scientific interpretation, comparison, uncertainty, or labeling. Do not penalize aesthetic design that preserves clarity.
